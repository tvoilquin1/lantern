# End-of-Life & Hospice

**Purpose:** What to expect in the final stage of dementia, how to recognize that the end is approaching, how to make decisions about comfort care, and how to navigate the dying process — for both the person and the caregiver.

---

## When Does End-of-Life Begin in Dementia?

Unlike cancer or heart disease, dementia has no clear "terminal phase" that a doctor can pinpoint. The disease progresses slowly, and many people live for years with advanced dementia before dying.

**Two things are true at the same time:**
1. Dementia is a terminal illness. There is no cure. Every person with Alzheimer's will die from it or from complications of it.
2. Predicting the timing is extremely difficult. People can remain in Stage 7 for months to years.

**The end-of-life phase generally refers to the last 6–12 months of life,** when the person's function has declined to the point where the focus shifts from managing the disease to ensuring comfort and dignity.

---

## Recognizing the Final Stage

The signs that a person with dementia is approaching the end of life are different from those in other terminal illnesses. There is no sudden turn. Instead, there is a gradual shutting down.

### Physical Signs

| Sign | What It Looks Like |
|------|-------------------|
| **Eating stops** | Eats less and less; refuses food; turns head away from the spoon; holds food in the mouth without swallowing. This is the body's natural way of shutting down — **not** hunger. |
| **Drinking stops** | Takes less fluid. May refuse water or have difficulty swallowing. Dehydration in the final days is natural and often causes less distress than forced feeding. |
| **Weight loss** | Dramatic. Muscle wasting. Arms and legs become thin; face looks gaunt. |
| **Sleeping more** | Spends most of the day asleep. Difficult to wake. |
| **Incontinence** | Complete loss of bladder and bowel control. |
| **Changes in breathing** | Cheyne-Stokes breathing: cycles of deep, rapid breaths followed by periods of no breathing (apnea). Congested, rattling breathing ("death rattle") — caused by saliva pooling in the throat, not pain. |
| **Mottling of the skin** | Purple or blue discoloration, especially on the hands, feet, knees. The skin feels cool. **This is a sign that death is approaching within hours to days.** |
| **Changes in circulation** | Hands and feet become cold to the touch. Nail beds may appear bluish. |
| **Changes in consciousness** | The person becomes increasingly difficult to rouse. They may appear to be in a coma-like state in the final hours. |

### Emotional and Behavioral Signs

- **Agitation or restlessness** — may pick at bedclothes, try to get out of bed, cry out. This can be a sign of pain, fear, or the natural agitation that sometimes comes with the dying process.
- **Withdrawal** — may stop responding to voices or touch. This is not rejection; it is the brain shutting down.
- **Terminal lucidity** — a brief period of clarity in the final days or hours. The person may suddenly speak, recognize people, or seem "back to normal." This is transient and does not mean they are recovering. It is often a sign that death is very near.

### What Is NOT a Sign

- **Not eating is not hunger.** The body naturally stops processing food. Forcing food causes choking, aspiration, and discomfort.
- **Not drinking is not thirst.** Dry mouth is uncomfortable, but this can be managed with mouth care, not IV fluids. IV fluids in the dying process can actually cause fluid overload, breathing difficulty, and swelling.
- **Withdrawal is not depression.** The person is not sad; they are preparing to leave. Treating this with antidepressants in the final weeks does not help.

## The Decision to Stop Eating and Drinking: A Caregiver's Guide

This is one of the hardest things a caregiver will face. The instinct to feed is primal. Watching a loved one refuse food can feel like watching them starve.

**What the research shows:**
- Artificially administered nutrition (feeding tubes, IV fluids) **does not** prolong life in advanced dementia
- Feeding tubes **do not** prevent aspiration pneumonia (they often cause it)
- Feeding tubes **do not** improve comfort or quality of life
- Feeding tubes **do not** prevent pressure ulcers
- Tube feeding can cause agitation (the person pulls at the tube), infection at the insertion site, and fluid overload

**What to do instead:**
- Offer food if they will eat, but do not force it
- If they turn their head, close their mouth, or hold food in their cheek — stop
- Focus on mouth comfort: ice chips (if they can swallow safely), moist swabs, lip balm, small sips of water
- If swallowing is difficult, ask the hospice nurse about medication options for dry mouth

**It is not starving. It is the natural end of the body's need for fuel.** You are not causing suffering by respecting their body's signals.

## Pain and Symptom Management in the Final Stage

People with advanced dementia cannot tell you they are in pain. You must watch for signs and treat empirically.

### Signs of Pain in a Non-Verbal Person

- Grimacing, furrowed brow, clenched jaw
- Moaning, groaning, crying out
- Guarding a body part (holding an arm or leg, flinching when touched)
- Restlessness, pacing in bed, trying to get up
- Changes in breathing (rapid, shallow, labored)
- Withdrawal or social disengagement that is new
- Crying without an apparent emotional cause

### The Hospice Pain Management Approach

| Pain Level | Treatment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Mild** | Acetaminophen (Tylenol) regularly |
| **Moderate** | Low-dose opioid (morphine, hydromorphone) at scheduled intervals, not PRN (as needed) |
| **Severe or continuous** | Around-the-clock opioid, often via concentrated liquid form placed in the cheek |

**Important:** Opioids in the dying process are not about addiction or sedation — they are about comfort. The doses used in hospice are carefully titrated to relieve suffering without suppressing breathing. Ask the hospice nurse to explain the protocol to you until you understand it.

### Other Symptoms Managed at End of Life

| Symptom | Intervention |
|---------|-------------|
| **Anxiety / agitation** | Low-dose benzodiazepine (lorazepam/Ativan) or antipsychotic (haloperidol, given sublingually or by injection) |
| **Respiratory congestion ("death rattle")** | Medication to dry secretions (scopolamine, glycopyrrolate, atropine). Repositioning the person on their side. The sound bothers families more than the person. |
| **Shortness of breath** | Oxygen (if it helps — sometimes it doesn't). Morphine reduces the sensation of air hunger. Positioning upright. |
| **Nausea** | Antiemetic (ondansetron/Zofran, promethazine) usually given as a suppository or sublingual liquid |
| **Constipation** | Bowel regimen (senna, Miralax, suppositories, enemas if needed) — especially important if opioids are used |
| **Dry mouth** | Moist swabs, artificial saliva spray, ice chips, lip balm, crushed ice on a damp cloth |

## What the Dying Process Looks Like (Hours to Days)

The actual process of dying from dementia is usually quiet. Most people do not suffer if symptoms are well-managed.

### Final Days

- **The person may not wake up at all** or only briefly
- **Breathing changes** — Cheyne-Stokes (cycles of deep breathing and no breathing) is normal and not painful
- **The person may make sounds** — moans, gurgles, sighs. These are mostly reflexive and do not necessarily indicate pain.
- **The person may move** — small movements of the hands, arms, or legs; picking at the sheets; opening and closing their mouth

### Final Hours

- **Breathing becomes irregular** — long pauses between breaths (15–60 seconds or more)
- **The breath becomes shallower** until it simply stops
- **The person is usually not conscious** at this point
- **They may briefly open their eyes** — this is a reflex, not a sign of awareness
- **The moment of death is usually quiet** — a final breath out, and then stillness

### What You Can Do

- Be present. Sit with them. Hold their hand.
- Talk to them. Tell them you love them. Say the things you need to say. Even if they cannot respond, hearing is the last sense to fade.
- Play their favorite music. Softly.
- Do not feel you must stay every minute. Take breaks. Eat. Sleep. Your loved one would not want you to exhaust yourself beside them.
- Call the hospice nurse if you are unsure about anything — that is what they are there for.

## What Happens After Death

### Immediate Steps

1. **Call the hospice nurse** (if on hospice). The nurse will come to the home, confirm death, and pronounce.
2. **Call the funeral home** you have arranged with (or choose one now if you haven't). They will come to transport the body.
3. **Call family and close friends** — or have someone do it for you.
4. **Do not call 911** unless the person was not on hospice. If 911 comes, they may be required to attempt resuscitation and transport the body to the coroner, which can delay funeral arrangements.

### If the Person Was NOT on Hospice

- Call 911 or the primary care doctor
- The doctor or paramedics will confirm death
- The body will be transported (by funeral home or coroner, depending on state law)
- If the death is unexpected or the person did not have a terminal diagnosis, a coroner's investigation may be required

### Practical Matters (First Few Days)

| Task | Who Handles It |
|------|---------------|
| Notify Social Security | Funeral home typically does this |
| Notify the bank and financial institutions | Family / executor |
| Obtain death certificates (get 10+ copies) | Funeral home orders from the state |
| Notify Medicare/Medicaid | Family |
| Cancel benefits and accounts | Family / executor |
| Notify utility companies | Family / executor |
| File life insurance claims | Family / beneficiary |
| File final tax return | Tax preparer |
| Arrange funeral or memorial service | Family |

**Get multiple certified copies of the death certificate** — you will need them for bank accounts, insurance, Social Security, the DMV, property transfers, and more. Most people underestimate how many they need.

## Grief: Before, During, and After

### Anticipatory Grief

Many caregivers begin grieving long before the person dies — often years before. This is normal. Anticipatory grief can include:

- Mourning the loss of the person you used to know
- Grieving the future you expected (retirement, travel, a shared old age)
- Feeling the loss of intimacy, conversation, and companionship
- Mourning the parent or partner who can no longer care for you

**Anticipatory grief does not mean you have given up.** It means you are acknowledging the losses along the way. It is part of loving someone with dementia.

### Grief After Death

Many caregivers expect to feel relief when the person dies — and then feel guilty when relief is the dominant emotion. This is normal. You have been under an impossible burden for years.

**Common post-death feelings:**
- Relief (that their suffering is over, that your caregiving is done)
- Guilt (for feeling relief, for not doing more, for wanting it to end)
- Emptiness (caregiving was your identity for so long — who are you now?)
- Numbness (you may not feel anything for weeks)
- Sadness (clean grief, without the complexity of caregiving)
- Loneliness (even if you were exhausted, you were not alone)

**None of these are wrong.** Grief has no correct shape.

### What Helps After a Dementia Death

- **Talk to other dementia caregivers.** They understand the complicated grief that others do not. Alzheimer's Association support groups continue for bereaved caregivers.
- **Write a letter** to the person you lost — about the disease, about your caregiving, about what you would have said if they could have understood.
- **Give yourself time to decompress.** Do not make major life decisions in the first 3 months (sell the house, move, quit your job).
- **Consider bereavement counseling.** Hospice provides 13 months of bereavement support free of charge — take advantage of it.
- **Let yourself feel relief without guilt.** You did an extraordinary thing. You do not have to pretend it was not hard.

### Complicated Grief

Some people experience prolonged, intense grief that does not lift. Signs include:
- Intense yearning or longing for the person that does not fade
- Avoiding reminders of the person
- Feeling that life is meaningless
- Numbness or detachment that persists beyond 6–12 months

If this sounds like you, seek professional help. Complicated grief is treatable with therapy.

## What to Say (and Not Say) to a Grieving Dementia Caregiver

| Say | Don't Say |
|-----|-----------|
| "You gave them such good care." | "They're in a better place now." |
| "I know how hard you worked for them." | "At least they're at peace." |
| "Tell me about them — what were they like before?" | "Aren't you relieved it's over?" |
| "I'm here. I don't need to say anything." | "I know exactly how you feel." (Everyone's grief is different.) |
| "What do you need right now?" | "You did everything you could." (They know. They may not believe it.) |
| "I remember when your mother..." (Share a positive memory) | "They didn't even know who you were at the end." |

**The best thing you can say to a grieving dementia caregiver:** "Tell me about them. What do you want me to know?" The person with dementia was a full human being before the disease took over. Let the caregiver remember them that way.

## Advance Care Planning Documents You Need Now (Before It's Too Late)

| Document | What It Does |
|----------|-------------|
| **Living Will / Advance Directive** | Documents the person's wishes about life-sustaining treatment — intubation, feeding tubes, CPR, dialysis |
| **Healthcare Power of Attorney** | Names the person who makes medical decisions when the person cannot |
| **POLST / MOLST** (Physician/Medical Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment) | A doctor's order that travels with the person — says "Do Not Resuscitate" and "Comfort Care Only." **This is stronger than an advance directive.** It must be signed by a doctor. |
| **DNR (Do Not Resuscitate) order** | Tells emergency responders not to perform CPR. Must be a signed doctor's order. |
| **Funeral pre-planning document** | Records the person's wishes about burial vs. cremation, service type, songs, readings, location, and who should be notified |

**Where to keep these:**
- Original on the fridge (visible to EMS)
- Copy in the medical binder
- Copy with the healthcare agent
- Copy with the primary care doctor
- Copy uploaded to the patient portal
