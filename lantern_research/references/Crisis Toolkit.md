# Crisis Toolkit

**Purpose:** What to do when things go wrong — fast. This is your cheat sheet for the emergencies that are specific to dementia caregiving. Keep a printed copy on the fridge and a digital copy on your phone.

## Agitation or Aggression — De-escalation

### Before You Do Anything

**Determine: is this a medical emergency or a behavioral crisis?**

- **Medical emergencies** need 911: the person has a weapon, is physically attacking you, is harming themselves, is having a seizure, has a head injury
- **Behavioral crises** need de-escalation: yelling, threatening (without a weapon), throwing things, trying to leave, refusing care

### De-escalation Steps (D.E.E.S.C.A.L.A.T.E.)

| Step | What to Do |
|------|-----------|
| **D**istance | Create physical space. Do not corner the person. Leave the room if you need to. Give yourself an exit path. |
| **E**yeball | Do NOT make direct eye contact — it's threatening. Look at their shoulder or past them. |
| **E**asy voice | Use a low, calm, slow voice — not loud. Your tone regulates theirs. |
| **S**implify | Use short phrases. "You're safe. I'm here. Let's sit down." |
| **C**hoose | Give a choice between two things, neither of which is "stop being upset." "Would you like to sit here or in the living room?" |
| **A**cknowledge | Validate the feeling. "You're angry. I hear you. I understand." Do NOT argue with the content. |
| **L**et go of control | You do not need to win. You do not need to be right. You need everyone to be safe. |
| **A**llow silence | Stop talking. Give the person space to settle. Talking more escalates. |
| **T**ime | This will pass. Most aggressive episodes last 5–15 minutes if you don't escalate them. |
| **E**xit plan | If it continues, have a room you can go to. Have a phone to call for help. |

### What NOT to Do

- ❌ Do NOT argue, reason, or explain
- ❌ Do NOT correct them ("I didn't take your wallet")
- ❌ Do NOT raise your voice
- ❌ Do NOT touch them unless necessary for safety
- ❌ Do NOT corner them or block their path
- ❌ Do NOT call 911 for non-violent agitation unless you are afraid for anyone's safety

### When to Call 911

- The person has a weapon (or anything used as one)
- The person is actively physically attacking someone
- You are injured
- The person is harming themselves
- You cannot control the situation and are afraid

**When you call 911, say:**
> "I am caring for someone with Alzheimer's dementia. They are in a state of confusion and agitation. I need help keeping everyone safe. Please send officers trained in crisis intervention."

This language matters — it activates Crisis Intervention Team (CIT) officers where available.

## Wandering — Person Is Missing

### Immediate Response (First 5 Minutes)

1. **Search your property thoroughly.** Check closets, bathrooms, basements, garages, under beds, in cars. People with dementia often hide in small, enclosed spaces.
2. **Check the most likely destinations.** Their former home, a familiar store, a church, a neighbor they know.
3. **Search within a 1-block radius.** People with dementia often follow their dominant-hand direction (right if right-handed) and stay within a few blocks.
4. **Call neighbors** — ask them to check their yards, garages, and property.

### If Not Found Within 10 Minutes

1. **Call 911 immediately.** Say: "A person with dementia is missing. They may be confused and unable to find their way home. They may not respond to their name." **Do NOT wait 24 hours.** The first hours are critical.
2. **Call the Alzheimer's Association Safe Return number** if enrolled: 1-888-572-8566
3. **Assign one person to stay at home** — the person may return on their own.

### What to Tell 911

Have this information ready in an envelope on the fridge:

- Person's name, age, height, weight, hair color, eye color
- What they were wearing last
- Any known medical conditions (diabetes, heart disease, seizure disorder)
- Whether they have a phone with them (and the number)
- Whether they are likely to respond to their name
- Any medication allergies or important medical info
- Recent photo (keep one printed and taped to the fridge)

### After the Person Is Found

- Do not scold or punish. They do not remember wandering and will not understand.
- Reassure. "I'm so glad you're safe. Let me get you a cup of tea."
- Review prevention measures: door alarms, locks, GPS tracker, Safe Return enrollment
- Call the doctor — a first wandering episode often signals a change in the disease

### Prevention (Before the Next Wandering)

| Measure | How |
|---------|-----|
| **Door alarms** | Simple chime alarms that sound when a door opens (<$20 on Amazon) |
| **Visual barriers** | Black mat in front of door (may look like a hole/drop-off); childproof doorknob covers |
| **Lock placement** | Slide locks placed high (out of line of sight) or low |
| **GPS tracking** | GPS watch or shoe insert; medical ID bracelet with Safe Return |
| **Neighborhood awareness** | Introduce yourself to neighbors; tell them the person has memory loss and may wander |
| **Police registry** | Some police departments have a dementia registry — register the person in advance |
| **Enroll in MedicAlert + Safe Return** | Alzheimer's Association program: 1-888-572-8566 |

## Fall

### If the Person Falls and You Are There

1. **Do NOT move them immediately** — unless they are in immediate danger (fire, water, traffic)
2. **Check for injuries:** bleeding, deformity of a limb, head bump, difficulty breathing
3. **Talk to them calmly:** "You've fallen. I'm here. I'm going to help you."
4. **If they can move and are not injured:** Help them sit up slowly. Then help them stand only if they are steady. If they are dizzy or in pain, do not move them.
5. **If they cannot move or are injured:** Call 911. Cover them with a blanket. Stay with them. Do not give them anything to eat or drink (they may need surgery).

### If You Find Them After a Fall (Unwitnessed)

You don't know how long they've been on the floor. This changes things.

- **Check for:** consciousness, breathing, obvious injury, head trauma
- **Check for hypothermia/cold exposure** — skin temperature, shivering
- **Check for dehydration** — dry mouth, sunken eyes
- **Suspect a head injury** if they are confused, sleepy, vomiting, or have unequal pupils. Head injuries in people on blood thinners (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban) are especially dangerous — ER immediately.

### When to Go to the ER After a Fall

- Loss of consciousness at any point
- Hit their head (especially on blood thinners)
- Unable to bear weight on a limb
- Obvious deformity, swelling, or bruising
- Severe pain
- Change in mental state from before the fall

### After the Emergency

- Notify the primary care doctor
- Review fall prevention: remove trip hazards, improve lighting, non-slip rugs, grab bars, physical therapy for balance
- Consider a personal alarm or pendant for the person (or a fall-detection smartwatch)

## Choking or Aspiration

### Signs of Choking

- Coughing forcefully, gagging, or making high-pitched sounds
- Difficulty breathing or noisy breathing
- Bluish color around the lips or fingernails
- Inability to speak or make sound
- Clutching at the throat

### What to Do

**If they are coughing forcefully:** Do NOT interfere. Encourage them to keep coughing. Stay with them.

**If they cannot cough, speak, or breathe:**
1. Call 911 (or have someone call)
2. Do the Heimlich maneuver (abdominal thrusts) — **adapt for a person in a wheelchair:** stand behind the chair, wrap your arms around, and perform thrusts
3. If they become unconscious, lower them to the ground and start CPR — chest compressions can dislodge food

### Preventing Aspiration

Aspiration (food or liquid going into the lungs) is a common cause of pneumonia in advanced dementia.

**Signs of aspiration:** Coughing while eating or drinking, wet/gurgly voice after eating, recurrent pneumonia, fever after meals, unexplained weight loss.

**Reducing risk:**
- Sit upright while eating and for 30 minutes after
- Thickened liquids (ask a speech therapist for the right consistency)
- Pureed or minced foods
- Small, frequent meals
- Cut food into very small pieces
- Avoid mixed-texture foods (soup with chunks, cereal that's both soft and crunchy)
- Have suction equipment nearby if risk is high (ask hospice to provide)

**If swallowing becomes a major problem:** Discuss with the doctor. Feeding tubes do not prevent aspiration pneumonia or prolong life in advanced dementia and can cause their own complications.

## Sudden Change in Mental State

A person with dementia who suddenly becomes more confused, agitated, sleepy, or "not themselves" is having a medical event until proven otherwise.

### The Acronym: D.E.L.I.R.I.U.M.

| Letter | Possible Cause | What to Check |
|--------|---------------|--------------|
| **D** | Drugs (new medication, dose change, or interaction) | Any med changes in the last week? New OTC or herbal supplements? |
| **E** | Electrolyte imbalance | Vomiting? Diarrhea? Not drinking enough? |
| **L** | Lack of sleep | How did they sleep last night? Multiple nights? |
| **I** | Infection (most common: UTI) | UTI is the #1 cause of sudden confusion in dementia. Check urine color, odor, frequency. Take temperature. |
| **R** | Respiratory (pneumonia, low oxygen) | Cough? Fever? Breathing fast? Lips blue? |
| **I** | Injury (especially head trauma from an unwitnessed fall) | Bruises? Limp? Holding head? On blood thinners? |
| **U** | Uncontrolled pain | Grimacing? Guarding a body part? Refusing to be touched? |
| **M** | Metabolic (dehydration, low blood sugar, constipation) | Last drink? Last meal? Last bowel movement? |

### Action Plan

1. **Take vital signs if you can:** temperature, pulse, respiratory rate
2. **Check for UTI:** color and smell of urine (if they haven't had a known fall)
3. **Check for injury:** any signs of a fall (bruising, limping, head bump)
4. **Call the doctor or nurse advice line** — describe the change and the possible causes you've considered
5. **If the person cannot be woken, has a fever over 101°F, has a head injury, or has difficulty breathing: go to the ER**

**Do not** dismiss a sudden change as "just the dementia getting worse." It might be. But you need to rule out a treatable medical cause first.

## Care Refusal — When You Must Act

Sometimes care is not optional — a medication must be taken, a wound must be dressed, or the person needs a bath to prevent infection.

### When It's Not Urgent

- **Back off and return later.** Try again in 15–30 minutes.
- **Change your approach.** Instead of "time to take a bath," try "let's run some warm water and soak your feet."
- **Change the person.** Ask someone else to try — the person may accept care from a different family member or a paid aide when they refuse you.
- **Change the environment.** Different room, different time of day, music playing.
- **Give a reason that matters to them.** "Your doctor wants you to take this so you don't get sick" may work. "Because I said so" won't.

### When It Must Happen Now (Medically Necessary)

- A wound is infected and needs cleaning
- A medication dose cannot be skipped safely
- There is a risk of hospitalization if the care is not done

**Techniques:**
- **Two-person approach:** one person distracts (talking, offering a favorite snack), the other completes the task quickly
- **Distraction items:** a phone with a familiar video, a photo album, a stuffed animal
- **Task chaining:** start with something they will accept (hand lotion) and transition to the task they resist
- **Call the doctor for advice** — some situations can be managed with a one-time medication order for short-term sedation (rare, last resort)

**Never restrain the person.** Restraint (holding them down, tying them to a chair, locking them in a room) is traumatizing, dangerous, and in many places illegal without a medical order.

## Suspected Abuse or Exploitation

### Recognizing Abuse

People with dementia are at high risk of abuse and exploitation because they may not understand what is happening or be able to report it.

| Type | Signs |
|------|-------|
| **Physical** | Unexplained bruises, burns, fractures; fear of a specific person; flinching |
| **Emotional** | Withdrawal, fearfulness, sudden change in behavior around a specific person |
| **Financial** | Missing money; unusual bank transactions; new "friends" who control access; missing jewelry; forged signatures |
| **Neglect** | Poor hygiene, pressure sores, dehydration, missed medical appointments, untreated medical conditions |
| **Sexual** | Unexplained genital injuries, STIs, bleeding, fear during bathing or toileting |

### What to Do

1. **If there is immediate danger, call 911.**
2. **Call Adult Protective Services (APS)** — every state has one. The Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116) can provide the number.
3. **Document your concerns** — dates, specific observations, what was said, photos of injuries (if safe to take them).
4. **If financial exploitation is suspected:** contact the person's bank (they have fraud departments), consult an elder law attorney, and file a report with APS.
5. **If the suspected abuser is a family member:** this is painful and complicated. APS involvement can provide an objective investigation and connect the family to resources. You do not have to press charges to get help.

**What not to do:**
- Don't confront the suspected abuser alone — this can escalate the situation
- Don't assume it's not happening because "they would never do that" — dementia changes family dynamics
- Don't wait for proof before calling APS — they are trained to investigate

## Caregiver Crisis — You

Your own crisis matters. If you cannot function, no one is safe.

### Immediate Steps If You Are Overwhelmed

1. **Put the person in a safe place** — a room with no hazards, with something familiar (TV, music, a snack)
2. **Go to another room.** Lock the door if you need to.
3. **Breathe.** 4 seconds in, hold 4, 4 seconds out. Do this 5 times.
4. **Call one person.** Your "I'm in crisis" contact. Tell them: "I need someone to come over now. I can't do this alone right now."
5. **If no one can come:** Call the Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline: **1-800-272-3900**. They have trained professionals who will listen and help you problem-solve in real time.
6. **If you are having thoughts of harming yourself or the person:** Call 911 or 988 (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline). Tell them you are a dementia caregiver in crisis.

## Crisis Kit — What to Have Ready

Keep a small bag or envelope by the door and one on the fridge:

**Medical info (printed):**
- Person's full name, DOB, blood type
- Diagnoses list
- Medication list (name, dose, frequency)
- Allergies
- Primary care doctor name and phone
- Neurologist name and phone
- Insurance card (copy)
- Medicare/Medicaid number

**Emergency contacts (printed):**
- Primary caregiver name and phone
- Backup contact name and phone
- Nearest relative name and phone
- Alzheimer's Association Helpline: 1-800-272-3900
- Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222
- Police (non-emergency): local number

**Physical items:**
- Recent photo of the person (for wandering/search)
- Change of clothes for the person
- Cell phone charger
- A few days of medications (if evacuating)
- List of comfort items (favorite music playlist, snacks, blanket)
