# Medical & Medication Overview

**Purpose:** A practical guide to the medical management of dementia — the medications, the clinical issues that arise at each stage, what to monitor, and how to work with healthcare providers.

**Important disclaimer:** This is an informational guide for caregivers, not medical advice. Treatment decisions must be made with the person's physician. Never start, stop, or change medications without medical supervision.

---

## The Core Medications for Alzheimer's Disease

There are two classes of FDA-approved medications that treat the symptoms (not the cause) of Alzheimer's disease. Neither stops or reverses the disease. They may temporarily slow symptom progression in some people — and in others, they provide no noticeable benefit.

### Cholinesterase Inhibitors

These medications increase levels of acetylcholine, a brain chemical involved in memory and learning.

| Drug | Brand Name | Approved For | Common Side Effects | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------------|-------------------|-------|
| **Donepezil** | Aricept | All stages (1–7) | Nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, loss of appetite, vivid dreams, muscle cramps | Most commonly prescribed. Generally well-tolerated. Once-daily dosing. |
| **Rivastigmine** | Exelon | Mild to moderate (3–5) | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, weight loss, dizziness | Also available as a transdermal patch, which has fewer GI side effects. |
| **Galantamine** | Razadyne | Mild to moderate (3–5) | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, dizziness, headache | Extended-release version available. Less commonly used than donepezil. |

**What caregivers should know:**
- The most common reason people stop these drugs is side effects (nausea, appetite loss). If this happens, ask the doctor about the rivastigmine patch or a lower starting dose.
- **"No effect" is common.** Many people see no improvement. A doctor may try them for 3–6 months and discontinue if there is no benefit.
- **Stopping abruptly can cause rapid decline.** If the drugs need to be stopped, it should be done under medical supervision, often by tapering.
- **Cholinergic crisis is rare but serious** — symptoms include severe nausea, vomiting, sweating, slow heart rate, and fainting. Seek emergency care.

### NMDA Receptor Antagonist

| Drug | Brand Name | Approved For | Common Side Effects | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------------|-------------------|-------|
| **Memantine** | Namenda, Namenda XR | Moderate to severe (5–7) | Dizziness, headache, confusion (sometimes), constipation | Works differently from cholinesterase inhibitors. Can be taken alongside them. |

**What caregivers should know:**
- Often combined with donepezil (a combination product called Namzaric exists).
- Generally better tolerated than cholinesterase inhibitors — fewer GI side effects.
- Can sometimes help with behavioral symptoms in addition to cognition, though it's not FDA-approved for that.
- Extended-release (XR) is once daily; immediate-release is twice daily.

## Medications for Behavioral and Psychiatric Symptoms

**Important first principle:** Non-drug approaches should always be tried first. Medications for behavioral symptoms in dementia have significant risks, especially in older adults. They are appropriate when the person's distress or safety risk outweighs the medication risk.

### Antidepressants

| Drug | Typical Use | Notes |
|------|------------|-------|
| **SSRIs** (citalopram/ Celexa, sertraline/ Zoloft, escitalopram/ Lexapro) | Depression, anxiety, agitation | First-line for mood symptoms. Citalopram requires dose limits (max 20 mg for >60 years) due to risk of heart rhythm problems. |
| **Mirtazapine** (Remeron) | Depression + poor appetite + insomnia | Useful when weight loss is also a concern. |

### Antipsychotics (Used with Extreme Caution)

| Drug | Typical Use | Black Box Warning |
|------|------------|------------------|
| **Risperidone** (Risperdal) | Agitation, aggression, psychosis | **Increased risk of death** in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis. Use only when non-drug approaches fail and the person is a danger to themselves or others. |
| **Quetiapine** (Seroquel) | Agitation, sleep disturbance, psychosis | Same warning. Often used at low doses for sleep (but not recommended for this purpose). |
| **Olanzapine** (Zyprexa) | Severe agitation, psychosis | Same warning. |
| **Aripiprazole** (Abilify) | Agitation, psychosis | Same warning. |

**What every caregiver must know about antipsychotics:**
- FDA black box warning means these drugs increase the risk of stroke and death in people with dementia. The risk is not theoretical — it is a statistically significant finding in randomized trials.
- They should be: **lowest effective dose**, **shortest possible duration**, **for a specific target symptom**, and **reassessed regularly** (at least every 3 months).
- If the doctor recommends an antipsychotic, ask: "What specific symptom are we treating? How will we know it's working? What is the plan to taper it off?"
- Always ask about non-drug approaches before agreeing to an antipsychotic.

### Other Medications

| Drug Class | Example | Used For | Cautions |
|-----------|---------|----------|----------|
| **Mood stabilizers** | Valproate, carbamazepine | Severe agitation, aggression | Less evidence than antipsychotics; side effects in elderly are significant |
| **Benzodiazepines** | Lorazepam (Ativan), diazepam (Valium) | Severe anxiety, acute agitation | **Avoid in dementia except in very limited circumstances.** Increases confusion, fall risk, and can cause paradoxical agitation (makes things worse). |
| **Sleep aids** | Trazodone, melatonin | Insomnia | Trazodone (low dose) is generally preferred over benzodiazepines or "Z-drugs" (zolpidem/Ambien). Melatonin is low-risk but limited evidence. |

**Bottom line on behavioral medications:** The safest approach is environmental and behavioral interventions first. Medications are a last resort, used when the person's distress or safety is at stake.

## Medical Issues Specific to Each Stage

### Stages 1–2: No routine dementia-specific medical management

- Annual physical exams with cognitive screening (MOCA, Mini-Cog)
- Cardiovascular risk management (BP, cholesterol, blood sugar) — this IS dementia prevention
- Hearing and vision assessments (untreated sensory loss accelerates cognitive decline)
- Baseline medication review — discontinue any anticholinergic medications (Benadryl, some bladder control drugs, tricyclic antidepressants), as they worsen cognition

### Stage 3: Diagnosis and Initial Treatment

- Formal diagnostic workup by a neurologist, geriatrician, or memory clinic (MRI, cognitive testing, blood work to rule out reversible causes)
- Initiate donepezil if appropriate (shared decision-making with doctor)
- Annual re-assessment of cognition and function
- Screen for depression (very common at this stage, both in the person and the caregiver)
- Driving evaluation referral — Stage 3 is the time to discuss and evaluate driving safety

### Stage 4: Increasing Medical Complexity

- Continue donepezil; consider adding memantine if progressing
- Start monitoring weight and nutrition closely (unintentional weight loss begins here)
- Evaluate for sleep disorders (sleep apnea is common and worsens cognition)
- Dental care becomes important — the person may resist brushing or forget to do it
- Annual vision and hearing checks
- Fall risk assessment — begin home safety evaluation
- Consider long-term care planning discussion with doctor (document capacity now)
- Vaccinations up to date (flu, pneumonia, shingles — avoiding infections is critical)

### Stage 5: Active Medical Management

- Donepezil + memantine is standard at this stage
- Close weight monitoring — unintentional weight loss is common. Consider nutritional supplements or referral to a dietitian
- Incontinence evaluation — rule out UTI (urinary tract infections cause sudden worsening of confusion in dementia)
- Pain assessment becomes difficult — the person may not be able to describe pain. Watch for behavior changes (agitation, grimacing, guarding a body part) as possible pain signals
- Dental: the person may be unable to cooperate with routine care. Ask the dentist about in-home or sedation options for necessary treatment
- Fall prevention becomes a medical priority — review medications that increase fall risk (blood pressure meds, sedatives, diuretics)
- Advance care planning discussion with primary care provider

### Stage 6: Managing Complications

- Swallowing evaluation (dysphagia) — choking risk increases significantly. Speech therapy referral can help
- Pneumonia prevention — oral care (brushing, mouth swabs) reduces aspiration pneumonia risk. Vaccinations critical
- Skin breakdown prevention — check for pressure sores, especially if the person is bedbound or chairbound
- Constipation management — common due to immobility, medication side effects, and poor fluid intake
- Seizures — may develop in advanced Alzheimer's. Any new, unusual behaviors or loss of consciousness should be evaluated
- Pain management — assume pain is present and treat empirically if the person is agitated and cannot communicate

### Stage 7: End-of-Life Medical Care

- Transition to comfort-focused care (palliative or hospice)
- Goal shifts from treatment to quality of life and pain/symptom management
- Dysphagia management — thickened liquids, pureed foods, or, if the person is no longer eating, discussion with the family about feeding tubes (evidence does not support their use in advanced dementia — they do not prolong life or improve comfort)
- Pain management — around-the-clock analgesia if indicated; watch for nonverbal pain signals
- Respiratory care — comfort measures for breathing difficulty
- Mouth care — frequent oral swabbing to prevent infection and discomfort
- Seizure management — antiseizure medications if needed
- Skin care — frequent turning and repositioning to prevent pressure ulcers

## Common Medical Problems in Dementia

### Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs)

**The single most important thing to know:** In a person with dementia, a UTI often presents as a sudden change in behavior (confusion, agitation, lethargy, aggression) — not necessarily with the classic burning or frequency. If the person has a sudden, unexplained behavioral change, **always rule out a UTI first.**

### Dehydration

- Contributes to confusion, falls, constipation, and UTIs
- Signs: dry mouth, sunken eyes, dark urine, low blood pressure, dizziness
- Prevention: offer fluids frequently. Use thickened liquids if swallowing is impaired. Offer preferred beverages (not just water).

### Constipation

- Very common due to medications (cholinesterase inhibitors, pain meds), low activity, low fluid intake
- Contributes to agitation and pain that the person cannot verbalize
- Prevention: high-fiber foods, adequate fluids, mobility, stool softeners if needed

### Pain

- Under-detected and under-treated in people with dementia
- Watch for behavioral signs: grimacing, guarding, agitation, restlessness, calling out, changes in appetite or sleep
- Assume pain may be present, especially with known conditions (arthritis, old fractures, pressure sores)
- Acetaminophen (Tylenol) is generally safe first-line. NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) have risks (GI bleeding, kidney injury) in older adults. Opioids require careful medical oversight.

## Working with Healthcare Providers: A Caregiver's Guide

### Building a Good Medical Team

| Provider | Role | When Needed |
|----------|------|-------------|
| **Primary care / Geriatrician** | Overall medical management, medication prescribing, coordination of care | Throughout |
| **Neurologist** | Diagnosis, specialized management, interpretation of imaging | Diagnosis + periodic follow-up |
| **Geriatric psychiatrist** | Behavioral symptoms, medication management for mood/behavior | Stages 4+ if behavior is challenging |
| **Speech-language pathologist** | Swallowing evaluation, communication strategies | Stage 6+ |
| **Occupational therapist** | Home safety, adaptive equipment, daily activity strategies | Stages 4+ |
| **Physical therapist** | Mobility, fall prevention, strengthening | Stages 4+ |
| **Social worker / Care manager** | Community resources, financial/legal guidance, caregiver support | Throughout |
| **Palliative care / Hospice team** | Comfort-focused care, symptom management | Stage 6+, or earlier if needed |

### How to Make Medical Appointments Work

- **Bring a written list** of: current medications (name, dose, frequency), recent changes in the person's condition, specific questions, and any concerning observations
- **Go in with your list first.** Many doctors will address the caregiver's concerns before the person gets tired or overwhelmed
- **Ask for the visit to be split** — the doctor sees the person alone briefly, then you speak alone with the doctor
- **Write down the answers.** Bring a notebook or record the conversation (ask permission first)
- **Request electronic access** to the medical record (patient portal) so you can see test results, visit notes, and appointments directly
- **Schedule at the person's best time of day** — typically morning for most people with dementia

### Questions to Ask at Every Major Visit

1. **"What stage is the person in, based on what you're seeing?"**
2. **"Are there any medication changes we should consider?"**
3. **"What symptoms should we watch for as the next sign of progression?"**
4. **"Is there anything we should be screening for now?"** (weight loss, swallowing, depression, falls)
5. **"Do we need any referrals?"** (PT, OT, speech, social work, palliative care)
6. **"What do we do if there's a sudden change?"** (when to call vs. go to ER)
7. **"Is hospice appropriate at this point?"** (ask earlier than you think you should)

## Medications to Avoid or Use with Caution in Dementia

| Drug Class | Examples | Why Avoid |
|-----------|---------|-----------|
| **Anticholinergics** | Diphenhydramine (Benadryl), oxybutynin (Ditropan), amitriptyline | Worsen cognition, increase confusion. Strongly associated with increased dementia risk. |
| **Benzodiazepines** | Diazepam (Valium), lorazepam (Ativan), alprazolam (Xanax) | Increase confusion, fall risk, paradoxical agitation, respiratory depression |
| **Z-drugs** | Zolpidem (Ambien), eszopiclone (Lunesta) | Same risks as benzodiazepines; no safer despite common belief |
| **First-generation antipsychotics** | Haloperidol (Haldol) | **Highest risk of death** in dementia patients. Avoid unless absolutely necessary. |
| **Tricyclic antidepressants** | Amitriptyline, nortriptyline | Anticholinergic effects worsen cognition |
| **Strong painkillers without bowel regimen** | Opioids | Cause constipation, confusion, sedation. If needed, always use with a bowel regimen. |

## Monitoring and Tracking at Home

Consider keeping a simple log of:

- **Weight** — weekly. Any loss of 5% in a month is concerning.
- **Appetite** — are they eating less? Full meals vs. picking at food?
- **Sleep** — hours per night, nighttime awakenings, daytime napping
- **Pain behaviors** — grimacing, guarding, change in mood, agitation
- **Bowel movements** — frequency, consistency, constipation
- **Fall incidents** — where, when, circumstances, injury
- **Medication changes** — new meds, dose changes, skipped doses
- **Behavioral changes** — new agitation, withdrawal, paranoia, aggression
- **UTI signs** — sudden change in confusion level, behavior, or continence

Bring this log to every medical appointment. It is often more useful than anything measured in the office.
