# Navigating the Healthcare System

**Purpose:** A practical guide to getting the care you need — medical appointments, insurance, home health, residential facilities, and working with the system that is not designed to be easy to navigate.

---

## Understanding the Landscape

The U.S. healthcare system for dementia care is fragmented, confusing, and expensive. There is no single "dementia care program." Instead, care is delivered through a patchwork of providers and payers, each with different rules, forms, and eligibility requirements.

**Your job as a caregiver is not to understand it all — it's to find the right navigator and ask the right questions.**

### Key Distinctions to Understand

**Medicare ≠ Medicaid.** They are different programs with different rules.
- **Medicare** is federal health insurance for people 65+ (or with certain disabilities). It covers acute medical care but **does not cover long-term custodial care** (the kind of care people with dementia need most).
- **Medicaid** is a joint federal-state program for people with limited income and assets. It **does cover long-term care** — nursing homes, and in some states, home and community-based services. But eligibility is strict and varies by state.

**Skilled care ≠ Custodial care.** This distinction determines what Medicare pays for.
- **Skilled care** requires a nurse or therapist (wound care, IV meds, physical therapy after a fall). Medicare covers this.
- **Custodial care** is help with daily activities (bathing, dressing, feeding, toileting). Medicare **does not** cover this. This is the care people with dementia need most.

## Finding a Doctor

### What Kind of Doctor Do You Need?

| Doctor Type | Best For | Availability |
|-------------|----------|-------------|
| **Geriatrician** | Comprehensive care for older adults, knows dementia | Scarce (geriatrician shortage — may have waitlists) |
| **Neurologist** | Diagnosis, medication management, imaging | Widely available; may have wait times for new patients |
| **Geriatric Psychiatrist** | Behavioral symptoms, depression, anxiety | Less common; worth seeking for challenging behaviors |
| **Primary Care (Internal or Family Medicine)** | Routine care, coordination, referrals | Most accessible; may need to educate on dementia specifics |

**What you need from a doctor — the non-negotiables:**
- Willingness to spend time with you (not 10-minute slots)
- Openness to discussing advance care planning and end-of-life decisions
- Knowledge of dementia medications and their limitations
- Responsiveness to phone calls (or a good nurse line)

**Questions to ask when choosing a doctor:**
1. "How many patients with dementia do you see?"
2. "How do you handle phone calls between visits?"
3. "Are you comfortable discussing end-of-life planning and hospice?"
4. "Do you have a care coordinator or social worker in the practice?"
5. "Can I be listed as a contact on the patient portal?"

## Home Health Care

Home health is a specific Medicare benefit — not to be confused with personal care or homemaker services.

### Medicare-Covered Home Health

To qualify, the person must be:
- Under a doctor's care
- **Homebound** (leaving home requires considerable effort, and they typically do not)
- Needing skilled care (nursing, physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy)

**What it covers:**
- Skilled nursing (wound care, medication management, catheter care)
- Physical, occupational, and speech therapy
- Home health aide for **personal care** (bathing, dressing) — but only while skilled care is also being provided. The aide hours are limited (usually 2–4 hours per day, a few days per week).
- Medical social worker visits

**What it does NOT cover:**
- 24/7 care
- Meal preparation, cleaning, laundry, transportation
- Companionship or supervision
- Long-term personal care (once the skilled need ends, the aide benefit ends)

**How to get it:**
1. Ask the doctor for a home health referral
2. The doctor orders specific services (nursing, PT, OT)
3. A home health agency evaluates the person and develops a plan of care
4. Medicare requires recertification every 60 days

### Private Pay Home Care (Non-Medical)

For the care that Medicare doesn't cover — bathing, dressing, companionship, supervision — you hire privately.

| Type | Typical Cost (2025) | Notes |
|------|-------------------|-------|
| Home health aide (personal care) | $25–$35/hr | Often 4-hour minimum shifts |
| Companion (non-medical) | $20–$30/hr | Supervision, conversation, light housekeeping |
| Live-in caregiver | $200–$350/day | Room and board plus payment |
| 24/7 care (two shifts) | $500–$800/day | Two 12-hour shifts |

**How to find help:**
- **Agencies:** Handle hiring, payroll, taxes, backup coverage. More expensive but lower administrative burden.
- **Private (direct) hire:** You hire someone directly. Cheaper but you handle taxes, payroll, background checks, and have no backup if they call in sick.
- **Home care registries:** Online platforms that connect families with caregivers (Care.com, HomeCare.com). Vet references.
- **Word of mouth:** Often the best — other caregivers, social workers, church communities.

## Residential Care Options

### At What Point Do You Consider Residential Care?

Honest signs it may be time:
- You cannot manage the person's safety at home (wandering, falls)
- The person requires 24/7 supervision and you cannot provide it
- Your health is deteriorating
- You cannot manage incontinence (or the person's hygiene needs)
- Challenging behaviors (aggression, severe paranoia) exceed what you can handle
- The person needs skilled nursing care you cannot provide
- You are exhausted, isolated, and at the end of your ability to cope

### Types of Facilities

| Type | Description | Typical Monthly Cost (2025) | Payer Sources |
|------|-------------|---------------------------|--------------|
| **Assisted Living** | Apartment-style living with meals, housekeeping, social activities, and some personal care. **Not designed for advanced dementia.** | $3,500–$7,000+ | Private pay, long-term care insurance, some states have Medicaid waivers |
| **Memory Care** | Secured unit within assisted living or a stand-alone facility. Staff trained in dementia care. Structured programming, secure wandering paths. | $5,000–$8,000+ | Private pay, long-term care insurance, some Medicaid waivers |
| **Nursing Home (Skilled Nursing Facility)** | Highest level of care outside a hospital. 24/7 nursing. Covers skilled and custodial care. | $8,000–$12,000+ (semi-private) | Medicare (short-term only), Medicaid (long-term), private pay |
| **Board & Care (Residential Care Home)** | Small group home (4–10 residents). Less institutional. More like a family home. | $3,000–$6,000 | Private pay, some Medicaid |
| **Continuing Care Retirement Community (CCRC)** | Multi-level campus: independent → assisted → nursing. Entry fee + monthly. Must enter while still independent. | $100,000–$500,000 entry + $2,000–$5,000/mo | Private pay, long-term care insurance |

### How to Choose a Memory Care Facility

| Factor | What to Look For | Red Flags |
|--------|-----------------|-----------|
| **Staff-to-resident ratio** | 1:5 or better during daytime | 1:10+ ratio; overreliance on agency/temp staff |
| **Staff training** | All staff trained in dementia-specific care, not just nurses | Only nurses have dementia training |
| **Safety** | Secured exits, door alarms, wander management system | Open doors, no alarm system, no wander prevention |
| **Activities** | Meaningful, dementia-appropriate activities (music, art, sensory); not just bingo | TV on all day; no structured programming |
| **Meals** | Flexible dining times, finger foods as needed, assistance available | Fixed meal times only; no modified food options |
| **Philosophy of care** | Person-centered, non-pharmacological approach to behavior | "We medicate when they're agitated" |
| **Cleanliness and smell** | Clean, well-maintained. Mild institutional smell is normal; strong urine odor is a red flag. | Strong urine or feces smell; residents left in soiled clothing |
| **Residents** | Look at the residents when you visit — are they engaged? Comfortable? Well-groomed? | Residents isolated, sleeping in chairs, appearing uncared for |
| **Visiting policy** | Open visiting hours; family encouraged to participate | Restricted visiting hours; reluctance to let you visit without notice |

**Tour check:** Visit unannounced at least once. Visit at different times of day (meal time, evening, weekend). If the facility resists, that's your answer.

### Paying for Residential Care

Most people pay out of pocket until they qualify for Medicaid. The reality:

- **Average stay in memory care is 2–3 years.** Total cost can easily exceed $150,000–$250,000.
- **Medicare does not pay for long-term care.** Period.
- **Long-term care insurance** can cover a significant portion — but only if the person has a policy. Most do not.
- **Medicaid** is the only public program that covers long-term custodial care. But the person must meet strict income and asset limits, which vary by state.
- **Medicaid planning** — transferring assets to meet Medicaid limits — must be done carefully. There's a 5-year lookback period. Transfers made within 5 years of applying can trigger a penalty period. **This is not a DIY project. Hire an elder law attorney.**

## Hospice: When and How

Hospice is **not giving up.** It is a specific Medicare benefit for people with a prognosis of 6 months or less (if the disease runs its expected course). For dementia, proving a 6-month prognosis is harder than for cancer — but many people with advanced dementia qualify.

### Hospice Eligibility for Dementia

Medicare criteria for hospice eligibility in dementia requires the person to meet ALL of these:

1. **Late-stage functional decline** — the person can no longer walk, dress, bathe, or toilet independently
2. **AND one of the following in the past 12 months:**
   - Aspiration pneumonia
   - Pyelonephritis (kidney infection)
   - Septicemia (blood infection)
   - Decubitus ulcers (pressure sores / bedsores) — grade 3 or 4
   - Fever recurring after antibiotics
   - Inability to maintain sufficient fluid and calorie intake (10% weight loss in 6 months)

**Real-world note:** Many people with dementia who do not meet these strict criteria still need hospice-level care for comfort. Some hospices are more flexible than others. If one says no, try another.

### What Hospice Provides

| Service | What It Means |
|---------|---------------|
| **Nursing visits** | A nurse comes 1–3 times per week, on call 24/7 |
| **Home health aide** | 2–4 hours of personal care, 2–4 days/week |
| **Medical equipment** | Hospital bed, wheelchair, oxygen, suction, commode — delivered to the home |
| **Medications** | Medications related to the terminal diagnosis (pain, nausea, anxiety, breathing) |
| **Social worker** | Emotional support, resource coordination, advance care planning |
| **Chaplain / Spiritual care** | Available for the person and family |
| **Bereavement support** | 13 months of support after the person dies |
| **Respite care** | 5 days of inpatient respite per benefit period (to give the caregiver a break) |

**What hospice does NOT provide:**
- 24/7 care at home (the family is still the primary caregiver)
- Treatment aimed at curing the dementia or other conditions unrelated to the terminal diagnosis
- Long-term personal care beyond the aide hours

**How to start:** Ask the doctor for a hospice referral. You can also call a hospice agency directly and ask them to do an evaluation.

## Care Coordination: Getting It All to Work Together

No one will coordinate your care for you. That's your job — or you pay someone to do it.

### Hiring a Geriatric Care Manager

A geriatric care manager (also called a "patient advocate" or "aging life care professional") can:
- Assess the person's needs and recommend services
- Coordinate doctors, home health, and facilities
- Navigate insurance and Medicare
- Advise on level of care and timing of transitions
- Provide crisis intervention

**Cost:** $100–$200/hour. Worth it for complex situations, especially when family members live far away.

**Where to find:** Aging Life Care Association (aginglifecare.org)

### Tips for Managing the System

1. **Keep a medical binder.** Print a binder with: advance directives, medication list, doctor contact info, insurance cards, visit notes, test results. Bring it to every appointment.
2. **Get everything in writing.** After every doctor's visit, ask for a written summary. Request that the doctor send notes to the patient portal.
3. **Be the squeaky wheel.** If you can't get an appointment for 3 months, call the office manager. Say: "This is a person with progressive dementia. A delay in evaluation could mean missing a treatment window." Persistence pays.
4. **Use the patient portal.** Message your doctor through the portal (faster than phone calls). Save all messages for your records.
5. **Know who to escalate to.** Doctor's office manager → Practice administrator → Hospital patient relations → State medical board. Usually you won't get past step 1, but knowing the chain helps.
6. **Ask about every discount and waiver.** Hospitals and doctor's offices have charity care programs. Drug companies have patient assistance programs. You often have to ask.
7. **Prepare for transitions.** Moving from home to hospital to rehab to home to memory care — each transition is a risk point for lost information, medication errors, and decreased function. Advocate especially hard at transitions.

## Quick Reference: When to Call Which Number

| Situation | Call |
|-----------|------|
| Medical emergency (chest pain, difficulty breathing, severe bleeding, head injury) | 911 |
| Sudden change in mental state (worse than baseline) | Doctor's office or nurse advice line |
| New fever over 101°F | Doctor or on-call service |
| New medication side effect | Pharmacist or doctor |
| Need home health | Doctor for referral |
| Need food, transportation, or other basic support | Area Agency on Aging / Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116) |
| Need legal or financial help | Elder law attorney |
| Need emotional support for *you* | Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline (1-800-272-3900) |
| Suspected abuse or neglect | Adult Protective Services (via Eldercare Locator) |
| Unsure where to start | Alzheimer's Association Helpline or Eldercare Locator |
