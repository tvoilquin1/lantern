# Communication Guide by Stage

**Purpose:** How to speak, listen, and connect with a person living with dementia — tailored to each stage of the disease. What works at Stage 3 will fail at Stage 6. This guide gives you the right approach for where they are now.

---

## The Golden Rule of Dementia Communication

The person is not being difficult. Their brain is processing a world that no longer makes sense to them. Your job is to enter their reality — not force them into yours.

**Three foundations that never change (just the execution does):**

1. **Assume they understand more than they can show.** Even in late stages, emotional recognition persists.
2. **Your tone matters more than your words.** Calm, warm, unhurried. The person reads your emotional state before they process your speech.
3. **Connection is the goal, not information transfer.** If they feel heard and safe, the conversation was a success — regardless of whether they remember it.

---

## Stage 1: No Impairment

**Communication ability:** Fully intact. No deficits.

**Approach:** Normal conversation. No modifications needed. If the person has expressed subjective memory concerns, listen without dismissing, but treat the interaction as you would with anyone else.

**What to avoid:** Minimizing their concerns ("Oh, it's just aging — I forget things too!"). Instead: "I hear you. Let's keep an eye on it together."

---

## Stage 2: Very Mild Decline

**Communication ability:** Subtle word-finding difficulty. Mildly slower processing. The person is aware of their lapses and may be anxious about them.

**What helps:**
- Be patient. Don't finish their sentences or supply the word unless asked.
- Give extra time to respond — 3-5 seconds instead of the usual conversational pause.
- Use normal adult language and topics. Do not condescend or simplify.

**What to avoid:**
- Drawing attention to memory lapses ("You already told me that," "I just told you").
- Testing them ("Do you remember what we talked about yesterday?") — this creates anxiety without benefit.
- Dismissing their self-awareness. They know something is off.

**Sample shift in approach:**
- Instead of: "We discussed this yesterday. Don't you remember?"
- Try: "I know this is a lot to take in. Let me repeat the key points."

---

## Stage 3: Mild Cognitive Decline (MCI)

**Communication ability:** Objective deficits detectable on testing. May repeat questions, lose thread of conversation, and have trouble following complex discussions. The person may withdraw from social situations they used to enjoy for fear of embarrassment.

**What helps:**
- **One idea at a time.** Don't layer multiple questions or instructions.
- **Reduce distractions.** Turn off the TV, move to a quieter room.
- **Use written backup.** Write down key information — appointment times, names, instructions. The written reminder persists when spoken words fade.
- **Check for understanding.** "Does that make sense?" instead of "Do you understand me?"
- **Avoid open-ended questions.** "What do you want for dinner?" is overwhelming. "Would you like chicken or fish?" is manageable.

**What to avoid:**
- Multi-step instructions ("Go upstairs, take a shower, put on the blue shirt, and meet me in the kitchen").
- Abstract concepts, sarcasm, metaphors. The brain processes literal meaning more reliably.
- Rushing or interrupting. Processing speed is slower.

**When the person repeats themselves:**
- Respond as if it's the first time. Gently. Redirect instead of correcting.
- "You already told me that" causes shame and confusion. "That's a good point" followed by a subject change works better.

---

## Stage 4: Moderate Decline (Mild Dementia)

**Communication ability:** Conversation is possible but increasingly difficult. The person may lose their train of thought mid-sentence, struggle to find common words, and become frustrated. They may withdraw from complex social interactions.

**What helps:**
- **Short, simple sentences.** 8–10 words max. "Time for lunch. Let's wash your hands."
- **One question at a time.** And only yes/no or two-option questions.
- **Name people and objects.** "Here comes your daughter, Sarah" not "Look who's here!"
- **Speak slowly and clearly** — but not loudly (hearing is usually fine; processing is the issue).
- **Use visual cues.** Point to what you mean. Show the fork, the coat, the bathroom door.
- **Allow extra time.** The person may take 10–15 seconds to process and respond.

**What to avoid:**
- Arguing or correcting. If they say they haven't eaten when you know they have, don't fight. "I'll check on your lunch. Let's have a snack meantime."
- Asking "Do you remember...?" They probably don't, and being confronted with the gap is distressing.
- Rapid topic shifts. Give warning: "In a few minutes, we're going to get ready for bed."

**When the person is frustrated with their own communication:**
- Validate the feeling, not the content. "I can see this is frustrating. Take your time. I'm listening."
- If they can't find a word, offer a guess gently — and be okay with being wrong.

---

## Stage 5: Moderately Severe Decline

**Communication ability:** Major gaps. The person may be unable to recall recent events, where they are, or who people are. They can still speak in sentences but may lose the thread, use wrong words, or revert to familiar phrases. Paranoia and suspicion may color conversations.

**What helps:**
- **Enter their reality.** If they ask when their mother is coming to pick them up (and their mother died 30 years ago), do not correct them. "She's not able to come today. I'll take care of you tonight."
- **Use the emotional message, not the literal one.** When they say "I want to go home," they may mean "I feel unsafe here" — not a specific address. Respond to the emotion: "You're safe. I'm here with you."
- **Reduce language to essentials.** "Time for dinner." "Let's sit down." "Here's your coffee."
- **Nonverbal communication becomes primary.** Your facial expression, posture, and tone carry more weight than your words. Smile. Stay calm. Move slowly.
- **Validate, validate, validate.** Even if the content is wrong, the feeling is real. "You're scared. I understand. I'm right here."

**What to avoid:**
- Reasoning or logic. You cannot argue someone out of paranoia or confusion that is rooted in brain damage.
- Asking questions that require memory or judgment. "What would you like to do today?" is overwhelming. "Let's take a walk" is better.
- Correcting identity confusion. If they call you by their mother's name, let it go. Connection matters more than accuracy.

**Dealing with suspicion/paranoia:**
- Don't take it personally. The accusation is a symptom, not a judgment of you.
- Don't argue. "I didn't steal your wallet" escalates. "I'll help you find it. Let's check the drawer together" dissolves the conflict.
- Redirect to a positive topic or activity. Paranoia often fades when the person is engaged in something pleasant.

---

## Stage 6: Severe Decline

**Communication ability:** Very limited. The person may speak only a few words or phrases — often the same ones repeated. They may lose the ability to name familiar people, including their spouse or children. They can still understand emotional tone and simple, familiar phrases. Much of the communication is nonverbal.

**What helps:**
- **Use very short, familiar phrases.** "Time to eat." "Hello, sweetheart." "Let's sit down."
- **Use their name frequently.** It may be one of the few words they still recognize.
- **Rely on tone and touch.** A warm voice, a gentle hand on the arm, eye contact at their level — these communicate safety and love when words fail.
- **Sing.** Familiar songs from their youth can reach parts of the brain that spoken language can't. If they cannot speak, they may still hum along.
- **Accept silence.** Sitting quietly together, holding hands, can be a complete and meaningful interaction.
- **Simple choices are still possible** — if you're willing to accept that "no" may be nonverbal (turning away, pushing the spoon away).

**What to avoid:**
- Long sentences, complex ideas, or multiple instructions.
- Expecting them to remember anything you just said moments ago.
- Pressuring them to speak. If they can't find the word, don't fill it in — just move on.
- Speaking about them as if they aren't there. Assume they hear and understand on some level, even if they cannot respond.

**When the person is agitated and cannot explain why:**
- Check physical needs first: pain, hunger, thirst, bathroom, too hot/cold, uncomfortable position.
- If physical needs are met, consider overstimulation. Remove noise, dim lights, move to a quieter space.
- Speak softly. Slow your breathing. Your calm regulates their nervous system.

---

## Stage 7: Very Severe Decline

**Communication ability:** Verbal communication may be absent. The person may be unable to speak at all, or may vocalize sounds, moans, or cries that are not words. They may not recognize anyone by name or face. However, emotional recognition and awareness of presence often remain.

**What helps:**
- **Your presence is the communication.** Sit with them. Hold their hand. Let them feel you are there.
- **Your voice is a comfort.** Talk to them in a calm, warm tone even if they cannot respond. Tell them about your day, what you're doing, who is visiting. The content does not matter; the connection does.
- **Touch matters deeply.** Gentle hand-holding, stroking the arm or forehead, massage — these communicate care when words cannot.
- **Familiar music is powerful.** Songs from their early adulthood can reduce agitation and create moments of calm connection.
- **Assume they hear and understand your emotional tone.** Do not say anything in their presence you would not want them to understand. Do not argue near them. Do not speak as if they are already gone.
- **Respond to sounds.** If they moan, murmur, or make any sound, respond gently — "I'm here. You're safe." Even if the sound is not directed at you, the response tells their brain that they are not alone.

**What to avoid:**
- Assuming silence means no awareness. It does not.
- Speaking loudly or sharply — this is frightening, not clarifying.
- Forcing interaction. If they are asleep, let them sleep. If they close their eyes or turn away, respect that as their communication.
- Withdrawing from them just because they can't talk back. People in Stage 7 are often starved of human touch and presence.

## Communication Across all Stages: Quick Reference

| Situation | Don't Say | Try Instead |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| They repeat themselves | "You already told me that." | "I hear you. Let me think about that." + redirect |
| They ask about a deceased person | "They died 10 years ago." | "Tell me about them. What do you miss about them?" |
| They want to "go home" | "You are home." | "You're safe here. Let me take care of you." |
| They accuse you of stealing | "I would never take your things." | "Let's look for it together." |
| They forget what you just said | "I just told you." | Repeat calmly, same words, same tone |
| They can't find a word | "Come on, you know this." | "Take your time. I'm listening." |
| They misidentify you | "I'm your daughter, not your mother." | (Accept the role) "I'm glad to be with you." |
| They ask the same question repeatedly | "I answered that already." | Answer as if it's the first time, each time |
