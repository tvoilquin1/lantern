# Legal & Financial Planning Timeline

**Purpose:** A stage-by-stage guide to what legal and financial documents need to be in place — and what decisions need to be made — before the person with dementia loses the capacity to participate.

**The key rule:** Before diagnosis and through the early signs stage, the person *can* sign legal documents, make their own decisions, and articulate their wishes. By the Middle stage, this is no longer possible. **The early signs stage (MCI) is the last safe window for most legal planning.** The early stage is the last possible window for simple documents.

---

## Timing Overview

| Stage | Legal Capacity | Must Do By Now | Can Still Do |
|-------|---------------|----------------|--------------|
| Before diagnosis | Fully capable | — | Everything |
| Early signs (MCI) | Capable (with support) | Healthcare POA, Financial POA, Advance Directive, Will, Trust | Guardianship not yet needed |
| Early stage | Diminishing — may qualify as needed | Any documents not yet signed | Some simple documents possible if capacity confirmed by doctor |
| Middle stage (early-middle) | Likely lacks capacity | — | Guardianship through court |
| Middle stage (late-middle) / Late | No capacity | — | Guardianship through court (full) |

---

## Before Diagnosis & Early Signs: Foundation

**Nobody wants to plan for dementia at this stage — and that's exactly why you should.** When the person is still fully capable, you have the widest range of options and the person's full participation.

### Essential Documents

| Document | Purpose | Notes |
|----------|---------|-------|
| **Durable Power of Attorney for Healthcare** | Appoints someone to make medical decisions if you become incapacitated | Sometimes called Healthcare Proxy or Medical POA. Takes effect only when a doctor determines you lack capacity. |
| **Durable Power of Attorney for Finances** | Appoints someone to manage bank accounts, pay bills, file taxes, handle real estate | Distinguish from a "springing" POA (which only activates on incapacity) — a springing POA requires a doctor's letter certifying incapacity, which can be logistically difficult. A *durable* POA is effective immediately. |
| **Living Will / Advance Directive** | Documents your treatment preferences: end-of-life care, artificial nutrition, pain management, resuscitation (DNR/DNI) | Most states have standardized forms. Can be paired with the Healthcare POA into one document (varies by state). |
| **Last Will and Testament** | Directs distribution of assets after death | Critical if there are blended families, dependents, or specific bequests. Without a will, state intestacy laws apply. |
| **HIPAA Release Form** | Authorizes healthcare providers to share medical information with designated family members | Without this, doctors legally cannot speak with the caregiver about test results, medications, or prognosis — even with a POA in place. |

**Recommended timing:** Immediately after diagnosis or strong suspicion of a progressive dementia. Do not wait for a formal dementia diagnosis — early-stage MCI is the window.

### Key Questions to Ask and Document

Answers should be written, signed, and shared with all designated agents:

- Who do you trust to make medical decisions for you?
- Who do you trust to handle your money? (These do not have to be the same person.)
- Under what conditions would you want to stop life-prolonging treatment?
- Do you want to be at home as long as possible, or would you consider residential care?
- What brings you comfort? (Music, specific foods, religious rituals, being outdoors, certain people.)
- Are there people you do NOT want making decisions for you?
- Do you want to donate your brain to Alzheimer's research after death?

## Early Signs (MCI): The Last Safe Window

**This is the most critical planning stage.** The person can still legally sign documents, articulate preferences, and participate in decisions — but their window is closing. Once they lack capacity, everything shifts to guardianship (court-supervised, expensive, invasive).

### Priority Actions

1. **Get all before-diagnosis documents signed and notarized** — if not done already. Do this now.
2. **Consider a Revocable Living Trust.** This avoids probate (public, slow, expensive) and makes asset management seamless if the person becomes incapacitated. Critical if they own real estate.
3. **Review beneficiary designations.** Retirement accounts (401k, IRA), life insurance, payable-on-death bank accounts — these pass outside the will, so the named beneficiary overrides the will. Make sure they're current.
4. **Create a financial inventory.**
   - All bank accounts (institutions, account numbers, approximate balances)
   - Investment accounts and retirement funds
   - Real estate (deeds, mortgage documents, property tax records)
   - Insurance policies (life, long-term care, home, auto)
   - Debts (mortgages, credit cards, personal loans)
   - Passwords, PINs, security question answers
   - Safe deposit box location and contents
5. **Investigate long-term care insurance.** If the person has a policy, understand what it covers, how to file a claim, and when benefits trigger. Most policies have a waiting period. If they don't have a policy — they are almost certainly not eligible now due to the diagnosis.

### Financial Red Flags to Address Now

- **Credit card debt or unpaid bills** — get a clear picture while the person can explain their finances
- **Unusual spending or gifts** — may indicate exploitation or early-stage poor judgment
- **Missing or unreported income** — pensions, Social Security, rental income
- **Unsorted paperwork** — unopened mail, disorganized tax records, expired insurance policies

**Recommendation:** Hire an elder law attorney if the financial situation is anything beyond simple. Find one through the National Academy of Elder Law Attorneys (NAELA). The cost is small relative to the cost of a guardianship proceeding.

## Early Stage: Diminishing Capacity

The person may still have enough capacity to sign simple documents with a doctor's certification, but complex planning is largely closed off. The focus shifts to administration of existing plans.

### What to Do

- **Activate the Financial POA** if it hasn't been activated yet — ensure the agent can legally manage accounts and pay bills. Some banks require their own POA forms; call them now.
- **File for veterans benefits** if the person is a veteran. VA Aid & Attendance benefits can provide significant financial assistance for long-term care, but the application process is lengthy and requires medical documentation of need.
- **Apply for Medicaid planning if needed.** Long-term care is expensive. Medicaid (not Medicare) covers nursing home and some home-based care. The rules are complex, state-specific, and include a 5-year lookback period for transfers. An elder law attorney is strongly recommended.
- **Simplify finances.** Consolidate multiple bank accounts where possible. Set up automatic bill payment. Remove the person's name from joint accounts that the agent will manage alone.
- **Stop credit card access.** If the person is making impulsive purchases, limit their available credit. They may not understand why; frame it as handling the bills for them.

### Signposts That It's Time

- The person cannot explain what a document means, even if they can sign their name
- A physician's capacity assessment indicates they are not competent to make legal decisions
- There is active financial exploitation happening (new "friends," caregivers, family members gaining control)

**If these documents were never signed before or during the early signs stage, you are now looking at guardianship. See below.**

## Middle Stage Onwards: Guardianship

If the person did not execute a durable POA when they had capacity, and they now lack capacity, **guardianship (or conservatorship)** is the only legal path to manage their affairs.

### What Guardianship Involves

- **Petition to a court** (usually probate or family court)
- **Medical evaluation** to prove incapacity
- **Court investigator** interviews the person, family members, and sometimes the proposed guardian
- **Ongoing court supervision** — annual reports, accountings, financial reviews
- **Time:** 2–6 months, sometimes longer
- **Cost:** $2,000–$10,000+ in legal fees, plus ongoing court costs
- **Public record:** The person's affairs become part of the public court record

### Guardian of the Person vs. Guardian of the Estate

| Type | Scope |
|------|-------|
| **Guardian of the Person** | Medical decisions, living arrangements, daily care |
| **Guardian of the Estate** | Financial decisions, property management, bill paying |
| **Emergency Guardianship** | Temporary (30–90 days), granted when there is imminent risk of harm — obtained faster but expires quickly |

### Alternatives to Full Guardianship

- **Limited guardianship** — the court only grants authority over specific decisions (e.g., medical but not financial)
- **Temporary/Emergency guardianship** — for immediate danger (financial exploitation, unsafe living conditions)
- **Representative payee** — Social Security can appoint someone to manage benefit payments without a court order (much simpler)

**Bottom line:** Avoid guardianship by planning early. If you are already here, find an elder law attorney immediately.

## Financial Assistance Programs

| Program | What It Covers | Who Qualifies |
|---------|---------------|---------------|
| **Medicare** | Short-term skilled nursing, home health (limited), hospice | Age 65+ or disability. Does NOT cover long-term custodial care. |
| **Medicaid** | Nursing home, some home and community-based services | Income and asset limits vary by state. Requires spend-down in most cases. |
| **VA Aid & Attendance** | Monthly cash benefit for care costs | Wartime veteran (or surviving spouse) who needs help with daily activities. |
| **Social Security** | Monthly income | Age 65+ or disability. SSDI for those under 65 who worked enough quarters. |
| **SSI (Supplemental Security Income)** | Monthly income for low-income individuals | Very low income/assets. |
| **Programs for All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly (PACE)** | Medical, social, and long-term care services | Age 55+, eligible for nursing home level of care, able to live safely in the community. |
| **Area Agency on Aging** | Local resources, case management, respite, meals | Varies by location and funding. |

## Additional Documents to Have Ready

| Document | Where to Keep It |
|----------|-----------------|
| Birth certificate | Safe deposit box or fireproof safe |
| Marriage certificate | Same |
| Social Security card | Safe deposit box |
| Military discharge (DD-214) | With financial documents — needed for VA benefits |
| Deed to home / mortgage statements | With estate planning documents |
| Funeral pre-planning or pre-need contract | With advance directives |
| List of all medications and providers | With healthcare POA — share with all caregivers |

## Checklist: Is Your Legal House in Order?

- [ ] Healthcare Power of Attorney signed and notarized
- [ ] Financial Power of Attorney signed and notarized
- [ ] Living Will / Advance Directive completed
- [ ] HIPAA releases signed for all key family members
- [ ] Will signed and witnessed
- [ ] Beneficiary designations updated on all accounts
- [ ] Financial inventory compiled and shared with agent
- [ ] Elder law attorney identified (consult at least once)
- [ ] Long-term care insurance policy reviewed
- [ ] VA benefits applied for (if applicable)
- [ ] Medicaid planning discussed with attorney (if relevant)
- [ ] Safe deposit box contents inventoried
- [ ] Digital accounts documented (passwords, security questions, 2FA)
