# Stage 2 — Very Mild Decline

**GDS Classification:** Stage 2 — Very Mild Cognitive Decline
**Alternate Names:** Age-Associated Memory Impairment, Subjective Cognitive Decline (SCD), "Senior Moments"
**Duration:** On average ~15 years in otherwise healthy persons
**Care Dependency:** None

---

## Clinical Snapshot

The person feels that their memory is not what it used to be. They may forget names, misplace items, or have trouble finding words — but these lapses are subtle enough that family, friends, and coworkers do not notice them. A medical examination will not detect any objective deficits.

**What's happening in the brain:** Pathological changes continue to accumulate silently. The brain may still be compensating effectively (cognitive reserve), but the person is aware that something has shifted.

**Key distinction from normal aging:** Everyone has moments of forgetfulness. The difference in Stage 2 is that the person themselves notices and is bothered by a change from their previous baseline.

**Important nuance:** Many people with subjective cognitive decline never progress to dementia. Some plateau; others revert to normal. But for those who do progress, Stage 2 is the earliest point where the person can articulate what's changing.

**What the person may say:**
- "I'm just not as quick as I used to be."
- "Names are on the tip of my tongue more often."
- "I have to write everything down now."
- "It takes me longer to find the right word."

---

## What You'll See (from the outside)

From the caregiver's or family member's perspective: **very little.** The person still handles work, finances, driving, and daily life independently. The changes are internal, subjective, and easily dismissed as stress, aging, or fatigue.

**Signs to pay attention to (even if subtle):**
- The person mentions memory concerns more than once
- They rely more heavily on notes, lists, calendars, and phone reminders
- They avoid situations where memory might be tested (trivia, new social groups)
- They express frustration or embarrassment about "not being as sharp"

**What is NOT present:**
- Objective deficits on standard cognitive screening tests (like the MoCA or MMSE)
- Noticeable impairment in work performance, social function, or daily activities
- Disorientation to time, place, or person

## Daily Care Guide

Since no daily care is needed at this stage, the focus is on **brain health maintenance and monitoring.**

### Brain-Healthy Lifestyle (What to Encourage)

| Domain                   | Recommendations                                                                                                             |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Diet**                 | MIND diet: leafy greens, berries, nuts, whole grains, fish, olive oil. Limit red meat, butter, cheese, sweets, fried foods. |
| **Exercise**             | 150 minutes/week moderate aerobic activity (brisk walking, swimming, cycling). Strength training 2x/week.                   |
| **Cognitive engagement** | Reading, puzzles, learning new skills, musical instruments, language learning. Novelty matters more than difficulty.        |
| **Social connection**    | Regular social activities. Isolation is a risk factor. Book clubs, volunteer work, group exercise.                          |
| **Sleep**                | 7-8 hours/night. Treat sleep apnea if present. Poor sleep is linked to amyloid accumulation.                                |
| **Vascular health**      | Control blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar. What's good for the heart is good for the brain.                      |
| **Hearing**              | Treat hearing loss promptly — untreated hearing loss is one of the largest modifiable risk factors for dementia.            |

### Monitoring (for the potential caregiver)

- **Annual cognitive screening** starting at age 65 (or earlier if family history is strong). Ask the primary care provider to include a brief cognitive assessment.
- **Document baseline** — if you can, get a baseline MoCA or Mini-Cog score while cognition is still normal. This makes future decline measurable.
- **Track changes** — note any new patterns: increasing forgetfulness, word-finding difficulty, changes in mood or social engagement.

## Caregiver Preparation & Coping

**This is the most important stage for the person who may become a caregiver.** You have time, clarity, and the cooperation of the person you may one day care for.

### What to Do Now (Before It's Harder)

| Action | Why Now |
|--------|---------|
| **Have the conversation** | Talk openly about the possibility of future dementia. Gauge the person's wishes, fears, and preferences while they can articulate them clearly. |
| **Legal documents** | Durable power of attorney for healthcare and finances. Living will / advance directive. These require a competent person to sign. |
| **Financial review** | Understand their income, assets, debts, insurance coverage, and long-term care options. Identify an elder law attorney. |
| **Family meeting** | Get siblings, adult children, and close relatives on the same page. Who will do what? Who can be called on? What are the financial realities? |
| **Build your support system** | Join a caregiver support group now (Alzheimer's Association, local community). Establish relationships with a primary care doctor and geriatrician. |
| **Learn about the disease** | Read *The 36-Hour Day*. Attend Alzheimer's Association educational workshops. Know what's coming. |

### Emotional Preparation

- **Grief starts early.** Many caregivers begin grieving the person they know long before any visible decline. This is normal.
- **Fear of the future is valid.** It doesn't mean you can't handle it. Acknowledging fear now prevents denial-driven crisis later.
- **Role reversal will happen.** At some point, you become the parent of your parent. That transition is painful. Start processing it now.

---

## Transition Warning Signs (Moving Toward Stage 3)

Stage 2 can last many years. The transition to Stage 3 is signaled by:

- **Others start noticing.** A friend, coworker, or family member comments on the memory lapses.
- **Performance slips.** Trouble with complex tasks at work or managing complicated household matters.
- **Repeating themselves.** The person begins to repeat questions, stories, or statements within the same conversation.
- **Getting lost in unfamiliar places.** Navigation difficulty, even with GPS.
- **Denial or defensiveness.** The person becomes irritable when their memory is questioned.

When these signs emerge, it's time for a formal medical evaluation.
