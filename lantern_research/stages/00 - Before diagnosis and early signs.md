# Before Diagnosis & Early Signs

**Context:** This is not a Lantern care stage — it is background context for families who are at the beginning of the journey, before a formal dementia diagnosis, or shortly after one. Lantern's active support begins at the Early stage.

---

## What This Covers

Dementia does not begin with diagnosis. Changes in the brain can occur silently for years before any symptoms appear, and the early warning signs are often subtle enough to dismiss. This document gives families the context to recognize what may be unfolding before a diagnosis is confirmed.

---

## The Pre-Symptomatic Phase

At this point, the person functions normally in every area of daily life — work, social relationships, finances, and independent living. They have no complaints, and neither does anyone around them.

**What is happening in the brain (if Alzheimer's is present):** Pathological changes — amyloid plaques and tau tangles — may be accumulating silently, years or even decades before any symptoms emerge. This phase is detectable only through specialized imaging or spinal fluid analysis in a research setting.

**Important truth:** Most people in this phase will never develop dementia. The presence of brain pathology does not guarantee clinical decline. This is not a caregiving phase. It is a preparation window.

### If you have a family history of Alzheimer's

**For the person who may eventually need care:**
- Have the conversation about future wishes while cognition is fully intact
- Encourage a brain-healthy lifestyle (the "MIND" diet, regular exercise, cognitive engagement, social connection, sleep hygiene, blood pressure control)
- Consider documenting a baseline cognitive assessment with a primary care provider

**For the potential caregiver:**
- Educate yourself about the disease
- Build a support network before you need one
- Understand your family's medical and financial landscape

This is the only phase where advance care planning can be done without the weight of cognitive decline. Use it.

---

## Subjective Memory Decline

The first noticeable change is usually reported by the person themselves: they feel that their memory is not what it used to be. They may forget names more often, misplace items, or have trouble finding words — but these changes are subtle enough that family and friends don't notice, and a medical examination will not detect objective deficits.

**Key distinction from normal aging:** Everyone has moments of forgetfulness. The difference here is that the person *notices and is bothered by a change from their previous baseline.*

**What the person may say:**
- "I'm just not as quick as I used to be."
- "Names are on the tip of my tongue more often."
- "I have to write everything down now."
- "It takes me longer to find the right word."

**What is not present:**
- Objective deficits on standard cognitive tests
- Noticeable impairment in work, social function, or daily activities
- Disorientation to time, place, or person

**Important nuance:** Many people with these subjective complaints never progress to dementia. Some plateau; others revert to normal. For those who do progress, this is the earliest point where the person can articulate what's changing.

### If you are the potential caregiver at this phase

| Action | Why Now |
|--------|---------|
| **Have the conversation** | Talk openly about the possibility of future dementia. Gauge the person's wishes and preferences while they can articulate them clearly. |
| **Legal documents** | Durable power of attorney for healthcare and finances. Living will. These require a competent person to sign. |
| **Financial review** | Understand their income, assets, debts, and insurance. Identify an elder law attorney. |
| **Family meeting** | Get siblings and close relatives on the same page. Who will do what? What are the financial realities? |
| **Build your support system** | Join a caregiver support group now (Alzheimer's Association, local community). |
| **Learn about the disease** | Read *The 36-Hour Day* (Mace & Rabins). Attend Alzheimer's Association educational workshops. |

### Signs that something is shifting (requires medical evaluation)

- Others start noticing the lapses — a friend, coworker, or family member comments
- Performance slips in work or managing complicated household matters
- The person begins repeating questions, stories, or statements within the same conversation
- Getting lost in unfamiliar places, even with GPS
- Denial or defensiveness when memory is questioned

## Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) — Before Dementia

This is the stage where the curtain starts to part. Friends, family, and coworkers begin to notice difficulties. A detailed medical interview will detect problems with memory, concentration, word-finding, and planning — but the person can still function independently in most areas of daily life.

**Crucial distinction:** MCI is not yet dementia. Measurable cognitive deficits exist, but they do not significantly interfere with daily life. Many people with MCI never progress to dementia. However, when Alzheimer's is the underlying cause, this is the prodromal (pre-symptomatic) phase.

### What you'll see

**Cognitive changes:**
- Word-finding difficulty — trouble with names or the right word, especially proper nouns
- Poor retention — forgets material just read or conversations from earlier in the day
- Difficulty planning and organizing — trouble managing a calendar, planning a trip, cooking a multi-step meal
- Getting lost in unfamiliar (or recently unfamiliar) places
- Misplacing valuable objects with increasing frequency
- Repeating questions or stories — not yet constant, but noticeable

**Behavioral and emotional changes:**
- Anxiety — the person knows something is wrong and may be afraid of what's coming
- Irritability when pressed or tested about memory
- Social withdrawal — declining invitations, avoiding groups, stopping hobbies that require recall
- Denial — minimizing or explaining away difficulties; resisting medical evaluation
- Depression — reactive depression is common and treatable

**What is still intact:**
- Independent living (dressing, bathing, eating, household chores)
- Navigating familiar environments
- Ability to drive (with caution — driving evaluation is warranted)
- Managing personal finances (with greater effort)
- Memory for remote events

### Actions to take now (before Early stage)

This is the last phase where the person can meaningfully participate in complex decisions.

| Action | Deadline |
|--------|----------|
| **Complete all legal documents** | Before Early stage — Durable POA, healthcare POA, living will, HIPAA releases (see [[Legal & Financial Planning Timeline]]) |
| **Long-term care insurance review** | Now — coverage may still be effective |
| **Family care plan** | Written agreement on roles, responsibilities, and contingency plans |
| **Home safety assessment** | Occupational therapist home evaluation |
| **Driving plan** | Establish criteria for when driving stops, and a backup transportation plan |
| **Advanced directive conversation** | Specific wishes for future care: home care preferences, facility type, life-sustaining treatment |
| **Find your doctor** | Establish relationship with a geriatrician or neurologist specializing in dementia |

### The Six R's (from *The 36-Hour Day*)

| Principle | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| **Restrict** | Limit demands that exceed the person's capacity |
| **Reassure** | Provide comfort and calm in the face of confusion |
| **Reassess** | Continually evaluate what's working and what isn't |
| **Reconsider** | Your assumptions about what the person can and should do |
| **Rechannel** | Redirect troubling behaviors into safer or more productive activity |
| **Re-evaluate** | Your own emotional state and coping resources |
